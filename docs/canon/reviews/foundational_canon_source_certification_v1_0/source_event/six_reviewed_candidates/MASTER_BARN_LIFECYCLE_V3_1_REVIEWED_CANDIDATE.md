# Purpose and Constitutional Scope

This Model governs the operational lifecycle of a barn or equine facility as an active care environment. It defines how a facility becomes operationally ready, receives horses and people, conducts daily work, manages safety and resources, responds to disruption, transitions operators, and closes without losing continuity.

It does not own the physical-place ontology. The Master Facility Domain Model owns structures, spaces, assets, utilities, hazards, maps, and physical-location truth.

# Barn, Facility, Business, and Operator Distinctions

- A facility is the physical place; a barn operation is the active operating system conducted at that place.
- A business may operate one or more facilities and may provide only some services at a facility.
- A property owner, facility operator, boarding business, training business, lesson program, and employer may be different entities.
- A facility assignment does not create horse ownership, broad medical access, or financial authority.
- A change in operator does not create a new physical facility, and a change in property ownership does not automatically transfer customer contracts or records.

# Operational Lifecycle Stages

1. Concept, acquisition, lease, or operating-right formation
2. Planning, licensing, insurance, configuration, and infrastructure readiness
3. Pre-opening testing, staffing, onboarding, emergency planning, and horse-intake preparation
4. Active operations and daily care delivery
5. Expansion, renovation, service change, temporary closure, or operator transition
6. Incident, degraded operation, evacuation, quarantine, disaster, or recovery
7. Suspension, closure, decommissioning, record transfer, and historical archive

Lifecycle stage must support overlapping and partial states. A facility may remain open while a wing, arena, pasture, or service is restricted.

# Operational Readiness

## Minimum Readiness Domains

- Legal and insurance posture
- Emergency contacts and escalation paths
- Water, feed, fencing, shelter, ventilation, lighting, fire protection, and access controls
- Staffing, role assignments, schedules, training, and supervision
- Horse intake, identification, occupancy, care plans, and restrictions
- Biosecurity, quarantine, medication, and veterinary coordination
- Maintenance, inspection, equipment, utilities, and inventory
- Communications, notices, offline procedures, and incident reporting
- Financial setup, service catalog, agreements, and billing responsibility
- Accessibility, minor protection, safeguarding, and visitor management

EquineSync may track readiness evidence and exceptions but may not represent that a facility is legally licensed, safe, insured, accredited, or compliant unless the applicable verification and claim rules are satisfied.

# Daily Operational System

- Daily work must connect horse, location, instruction, responsible actor, timing, evidence, exception, escalation, and completion state.
- Tasks should support recurrence, delegation, reassignment, dependency, route or location order, acknowledgment, and offline completion.
- Normal work should be fast; exceptions, missed care, conflicts, and restrictions should be prominent.
- Shift handoffs must preserve unresolved work, urgent context, changed instructions, and responsibility.
- Announcements, messages, notices, and emergency broadcasts must use governed communication classes rather than informal duplication.
- Operational dashboards should answer what changed, what is late, what is blocked, who is responsible, and what requires escalation.

# Horse Intake, Occupancy, and Movement

1. Pre-intake relationship, agreement, and authority validation
2. Horse identity confirmation and duplicate prevention
3. Care, medical, restriction, emergency, contact, billing, and equipment intake
4. Space assignment and suitability review
5. Arrival condition, documents, quarantine, and transition evidence
6. Active occupancy and daily location management
7. Internal moves, temporary departures, shows, transport, hospitalization, or evacuation
8. Departure, transfer, access revocation, record handoff, financial reconciliation, and space release

Occupancy is temporal and must not overwrite prior assignments. Capacity and occupancy projections must distinguish confirmed, reserved, temporary, unavailable, quarantined, and restricted spaces.

# People, Workforce, and Provider Operations

- Employees, contractors, volunteers, students, interns, guardians, visitors, and providers require distinct relationships and access boundaries.
- Employment authority and platform permissions are related but not interchangeable.
- Staff should receive only the horse, task, location, and safety information needed for assigned work.
- Minors require guardian linkage, communication boundaries, supervision rules, and protection from unrelated data exposure.
- Provider access must be horse- and service-scoped, time-aware, revocable, and separated from unrelated barn, owner, staff, and financial information.
- Scheduling, timekeeping, payroll, performance management, credentialing, and discipline must not be conflated into one opaque workforce score.

# Safety, Emergency, Incident, and Degraded Operations

- Emergency plans must identify roles, contacts, locations, resources, animal priorities, communication channels, and fallback procedures.
- The system must support fire, weather, injury, escape, utility failure, biosecurity event, security threat, missing horse, transport incident, and human safeguarding scenarios.
- Degraded mode must clearly distinguish available, stale, locally saved, pending, failed, and unavailable information.
- Offline emergency packets must be limited, protected, current enough for intended use, and reviewed after reconnection.
- Incidents require attributable facts, evidence, corrections, restricted notes, notifications, follow-up, and claims handling without premature blame.
- Silent failure is unacceptable where horse welfare, medication, evacuation, security, or emergency communication may be affected.

# Maintenance, Assets, Utilities, and Inventory

- Maintenance work must connect to the affected facility object, condition, hazard, priority, responsible party, evidence, downtime, and completion review.
- Preventive, corrective, emergency, inspection, and capital work must remain distinguishable.
- Out-of-service assets and spaces must affect scheduling and occupancy where relevant.
- Water, electrical, drainage, ventilation, fire, gate, fencing, and access-control failures require heightened escalation.
- Feed, bedding, medication, supplies, fuel, and critical inventory require minimum levels, expiration, storage, restricted access, and reconciliation where appropriate.
- Photos and AI extraction may assist inventory and condition tracking but do not replace human verification.

# Biosecurity, Quarantine, and Health Protection

- Biosecurity status, exposure, quarantine, testing, vaccination, temperature monitoring, movement restriction, and release criteria require scoped access and provenance.
- Quarantine must affect space availability, movement, staffing, visitor access, shared equipment, and communication.
- The system may assist with protocols and reminders but may not diagnose disease or override qualified veterinary direction.
- Public notices must protect privacy while communicating necessary risk information.
- Conflicting or outdated health restrictions must be surfaced and reconciled before ordinary operations resume.

# Financial and Service Operations Boundary

Barn operations may create service records, billable events, occupancy charges, credits, deposits, or exceptions, but Financial Truth governs the authoritative monetary state.

- Operational completion does not automatically prove a valid charge.
- Nonpayment does not authorize withholding essential horse-welfare information or falsifying transfer status.
- Suspension and service restriction require clear policy, notice, safety review, and lawful handling.
- Boarding, training, lessons, and property services may be provided by different businesses at the same facility.

# Transition, Operator Change, and Closure

## Required Continuity Areas

- Horse and owner notification
- Current care and emergency handoff
- Access revocation and successor authority
- Staff and provider responsibilities
- Contracts, deposits, balances, and claims
- Facility records, maintenance, hazards, and inspections
- Data export, retention, private notes, and evidence preservation
- Open incidents, disputes, safeguarding matters, and legal holds
- Physical keys, devices, credentials, integrations, and external processors

A closure must not strand horses, erase history, conceal unresolved incidents, or silently transfer private data to a successor operator.

# Barn Analytics and AI Limits

- Operational analytics may identify missed work, capacity constraints, inventory trends, maintenance burden, and service patterns.
- Metrics must not become unexplained staff, horse, owner, provider, or facility quality scores.
- AI may summarize the day, draft communications, detect inconsistencies, and suggest priorities, but may not make final welfare, disciplinary, medical, legal, or employment decisions.
- Small-cell privacy and cross-tenant comparison rules apply to benchmarking and public claims.

# Lifecycle Success Criteria

- Every horse is identifiable and connected to current care and location.
- Staff can understand and complete the day’s work quickly.
- Exceptions and emergencies are visible and escalated.
- Physical-place truth and operational truth remain aligned but separately governed.
- Operator changes and closure preserve horse, record, financial, and evidentiary continuity.
- The barn can degrade safely when connectivity, vendors, utilities, or staffing fail.
